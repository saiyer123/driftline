The verdict

Claude the engineer, not Claude the gambler

  1. Trade slow, read deep. Swing trading US equities/ETFs over days–weeks, driven by an event-driven signal engine (earnings calls, filings, news catalysts). This is the one style where every constraint — LLM latency, retail data budgets, broker access, and the published evidence on LLM edge — points the same way.
  2. The LLM authors strategies and reads text; deterministic code trades. Every credible production system uses this split. Every "let the LLM trade tick-by-tick" experiment with real money mostly lost it.
  3. Three-plane architecture: a deterministic execution core with hard risk rails Claude cannot override, an async Claude cognition plane (research, strategy authoring, regime reading, post-mortems), and an event-sourced ledger feeding the live P&L dashboard.
  4. Paper first, promotion gates always. Backtest gate → paper soak → small live capital → scale, with strategy changes shipped as reviewable pull requests.
Part I · The landscape

What people are actually doing

The public record splits into three tiers: real-money autonomy experiments (mostly cautionary), engineering-first production systems (the success stories), and open-source reference frameworks (great scaffolding, simulation-only).

Real-money autonomy: the cautionary tier

Alpha Arena (Nof1.ai) gave six frontier LLMs $10,000 each to trade crypto perpetuals autonomously on Hyperliquid in late 2025. Four of six lost money; win rates ran 25–30% across all models; organizers reported early P&L was dominated by trading costs from overtrading, with one model paying $1,654 in fees on a $10K account in two weeks. Their conclusion: LLMs "don't really handle numerical time series data very well." Individual experiments rhyme with this — a well-documented Claude Code paper-trading bot beat the S&P over a month (+7% vs +4.5%) but with 22% drawdowns, and its author still refuses to fund it with real money.

Production systems: the engineering tier

The best-documented system running real money 24/7 is a Polymarket arbitrage bot ("Polyphemus"): 36,000 lines of code written and maintained entirely by Claude Code over four months, 99.2% uptime, ~$136/month in API costs. Its defining design decision: Claude makes zero live trading decisions. Deterministic thresholds trade; risk limits are human-owned constraints the agent cannot edit; Claude-generated code passes a two-gate review and shadow-mode deployment before touching capital (which cut its error rate ~6×, catching 8 silent bugs).

Reference frameworks

virattt/ai-hedge-fund (~63K stars) and the TradingAgents paper define the multi-agent pattern: persona analysts (fundamentals, sentiment, technicals, valuation), bull-vs-bear researcher debate, a trader agent, and a risk manager that can veto. Both are simulation-only, and a 2026 reproducibility survey of 19 LLM-trading studies found essentially none fully reproducible — the architecture patterns are the durable contribution, not the published returns.

The plumbing has standardized

MCP servers are now the glue between Claude and brokers: Alpaca ships an official one (free, API-identical paper trading), ccxt ships one for 100+ crypto exchanges (opt-in trading tier, notional caps, local keys), and half a dozen community servers cover Interactive Brokers. The convention everywhere: read-only by default, capped and confirmed trading opt-in.

The trap unique to LLM backtests Parametric look-ahead bias. A model trained through 2025 remembers what happened to markets before its cutoff, so any backtest with an LLM in the loop over pre-cutoff data is contaminated. Rigorous 20-year evaluations (FINSABER, KDD 2026; StockBench) find reported LLM trading advantages largely evaporate under honest testing. The only benchmark that counts is your own forward paper-trading record.

Part III · Architecture

The three-plane design

The same skeleton as production platforms like NexusTrade (agent sandboxes + elastic research compute + a small controlled-execution service + approval-and-risk gating inside the execution walls, all decoupled by an event bus), miniaturized to one-person scale. The rule that organizes everything: the LLM never touches the broker; it submits intents that pass through a deterministic gate.

Why this split wins

Three ways to wire an LLM into trading: author (Claude writes deterministic strategy code, the code trades — zero latency in the trade path, fully backtestable, failures are debuggable diffs), in-the-loop (Claude consulted per decision — seconds of latency, poor reproducibility, barely backtestable), and hybrid. Driftline is the hybrid: Claude authors and iterates strategies, and separately publishes slow-moving bounded parameters — regime: risk-off, risk_appetite: 0.4 — that the fast code consumes as clamped inputs. LLM output enters the trade path only as a bounded number, never as an order.

Safety rails (non-negotiable, learned from others' losses)

  • Risk gate outside Claude's reach: max position size, per-symbol and gross exposure caps, daily-loss halt (~3%), order-rate limits, market-hours enforcement — in code the agents cannot edit in the pipeline that runs it.
  • Layered kill switches: software halt, a flag every component checks, broker-level buying-power caps, and the capital ladder itself.
  • Approval at the right altitude: not per-trade (defeats automation) but per-strategy-change — Claude's edits ship as pull requests with an attached backtest report; a human merge is the gate. Audit, rollback, and diff-level explainability come free with git.
  • Ground truth is read-only: positions and balances come from the ledger and broker reconciliation, never from an agent's memory. Reconcile against the broker on a loop; halt on divergence.
  • Default to no-trade: uncertain or low-confidence agent output resolves to doing nothing.
  • Fee-awareness in every fitness function: overtrading and costs were the #1 documented killer of LLM traders; backtests must survive a 30–45% cost haircut.

The novel part worth being ambitious about

Overnight strategy evolution: Claude generates and mutates candidate strategy code, a vectorized backtest with walk-forward splits scores each candidate, survivors seed the next generation, and quality-diversity selection (QuantEvolve-style) keeps the population varied instead of converging on one overfit champion. Each morning the best validated candidate arrives as a pull request with its report. This loop — genuinely impractical in 2023, routine for a scheduled agent in 2026 — is the platform's distinctive capability. Its main risk is multiplicity (test thousands of candidates and some win by luck), mitigated by walk-forward splits, deflated-Sharpe corrections, realistic costs, and a paper-trading soak before promotion.

Part IV · Roadmap

Build order

  1. Skeleton on paper

    Event bus, ledger schema, Alpaca paper account wired through the risk gate, one deliberately boring baseline strategy (momentum rotation). Reconciliation loop and kill switches from day one.

    Gate: a week of clean paper fills, zero reconciliation breaks

  2. Dashboard v1

    Live equity curve, positions blotter, per-strategy P&L attribution, decision journal browser, kill-switch button. Grafana drawdown alerts. Built early so every later phase is observable.

    Gate: you can answer "why is it down today?" from the dashboard alone

  3. The reading edge

    EDGAR filings + news ingestion into the Parquet lake and pgvector; the Researcher agent starts publishing regime and per-name signals as bounded parameters; the event-driven earnings module trades the post-earnings drift window on paper.

    Gate: signal hit-rate tracked forward for 4–6 weeks, fees included

  4. Evolution loop

    Nightly Strategist agent: generate → vectorized walk-forward scoring → event-driven validation → morning pull request. Reviewer agent writes the daily post-mortem.

    Gate: an evolved strategy beats the baseline on paper, after costs

  5. Small live capital

    Flip the best paper-proven strategy to live with a small allocation and broker-level caps. Scale only what survives. Everything else stays on paper permanently — paper is not a phase, it's where all new ideas live.

    Gate: your call, per strategy, with the forward record in front of you
Appendix

Ground rules & sources

Claude builds, researches, backtests, paper-trades, and monitors this system; it proposes strategy changes as reviewable PRs and can run the automation under your broker keys and risk limits. Claude does not itself execute real-money trades or transfers, and nothing here is investment advice — the forward paper record, not this document, decides what goes live. Realistic goal: a modest, risk-managed edge and a dramatic upgrade in research throughput and discipline, not a money printer. Most retail algorithms lose money; the design above exists to keep this one alive long enough to learn.
